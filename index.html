Managing Resources

Essential strategies for effective kitchen and business management.

1. Conflict Management

Conflict is a fact of life. Interpersonal conflict starts in childhood, but in the workplace, it must be managed proactively.

Reasons for Conflict

  • Racism, sexism, differences in opinion.
  • Inappropriate personal habits, non-compliance with organisational norms/values.
  • Discriminatory behaviour, working conditions.
  • Unrealistic work expectations, personal antagonism.

Note: Formal procedures (grievances/disciplinary) can depersonalise conflict, converting it into a standard work-role approach that spells out who is to do what by when.

Conflict Between Employee and Manager

Often the result of poor communication regarding activities, progress, results, and achievements. Main issues include:

  • Failing to communicate accurately or promptly.
  • Going it alone (making decisions requiring approval).
  • Coming up with problems rather than solutions.
  • Feeling hurt when ideas are rejected instead of proposing alternatives.
  • Allowing disagreements to grow without limiting damage.
  • Failing to do what the job requires.
  • Balancing team expectations with your manager's demands.
Turning the situation round: Nine steps to enhance working relationships.
  1. Keep the manager well informed via regular progress reports.
  2. Inform them of problems and opportunities at the right stage.
  3. Ask for advice when needed; use them as a resource.
  4. Make proposals for action clearly, at the right time and detail.
  5. If a proposal is rejected, wait and put forward an alternative.
  6. Deal with disagreements positively. Avoid falling out.
  7. Continue to find ways to improve your relationship.
  8. Check you have completed everything required of you.
  9. Carry out activities positively, willingly, and helpfully.

Constructive vs. Destructive Conflict

Constructive: Acts as the first stage towards negotiating, clears the air, and helps staff talk.

Destructive: Produces name-calling, makes people angry, and causes withdrawal.

Reactions to Conflict

  • Physical: Sweaty palms, raised pulse, dry mouth, trembling.
  • Flight (Passive): Withdrawing eye contact, hiding, continual agreement, guilt. This is unsatisfactory.
  • Fight (Aggressive): Raised voice, pointing fingers, glaring, invading space, not listening. Equally unsatisfactory.

Why conflict arises: Lack of chance to discuss problems, breakdown in trust, misunderstandings about standards, failing to communicate correctly.

2. Team Leadership & Work-Life Balance

Leading the Team

  • Look at tomorrow's problems today to detect signs of change.
  • Embrace change and turn it to positive advantage.
  • Set high standards and clear objectives.
  • Create a sense of value and purpose in work.
  • Act decisively based on sound principles, not impulse.
  • Set the right tone through actions and beliefs.
  • Create an atmosphere of enthusiasm and fulfilment.
  • Define clear responsibilities so collective effort is enhanced.
  • Recognise what motivates each member.

Work-Life Balance

Integrating needs outside work with no detriment to work yields considerable business benefits.

Research Findings (DTI, 2001):
• A third of UK managers would change jobs for better work-life balance.
• 57% of students consider a balanced lifestyle their top priority.

3. Effective Communication

Communication is a shared responsibility. It requires a transmitter and a receiver. The greatest scope for improvement lies in listening, observing, reading, and watching.

Intent vs. Effect

Hearing the content is not enough. There must be a match between the 'intent' of the message and the 'effect' it has on the individual. Bridging this gap prevents misunderstandings and builds respect.

Planning Communication

Significant communication lines (those that recur frequently or carry high rewards/penalties) must be planned.

  • Content: Collecting data, ordering thoughts, formulating information.
  • Process: Alternative ways communication may proceed to achieve objectives.

Meetings

Note on written communication: Written instructions are excellent for tangible evidence, but must be specific, unambiguous, and not too wordy. Emails risk losing the value of face-to-face contact.

4. Managing Diversity

The hospitality workforce is becoming more diverse (women, ethnic mix, ageing population, mobility of labour).

What is Diversity?

Recognising that people are different. It includes visible differences (gender, ethnicity, age, disability) and less visible differences (sexual orientation, background, personality, work style). It is about harnessing difference to improve creativity and innovation.

Why is it Important?

Markets are changing (women and older people have more spending power, minority groups are important segments). The workforce must represent these markets. Proactive diversity management helps to:

  • Access the best people from the widest labour pool.
  • Develop creative talents of all employees.
  • Motivate staff and reduce labour turnover.
  • Improve quality and customer service.

5. Motivation & Team Dynamics

Key Theories

  • F.W. Taylor (1911): Scientific management. Broke jobs into simple tasks, isolated individuals, and paid bonuses for productivity. (Flaw: Treated people like machines).
  • Abraham Maslow (1954): Hierarchy of Needs (Physiological, Safety, Social, Esteem, Self-Fulfilment).
  • Frederick Herzberg (1959): 'Hygiene factors' (policy, management styles, pay, status). If absent, they cause dissatisfaction.

Motivating a Team

  • Make work interesting, challenging, and demanding.
  • Ensure people know what is expected and the standards.
  • Link rewards to effort and results.
  • Recognise good performance and intercede on behalf of staff.
  • Get to know staff well, understand their needs and aspirations.

Supportive Team Practices

Listening Skills: Pay attention, look interested, don't interrupt, build on proposals, ask for clarity, summarise to check understanding.

Cooperating: Encourage others to give views, compliment good ideas, avoid coercion, consider different proposals, offer new ideas openly.

6. Managing Your Manager

Developing trust and support with your immediate manager will not happen by accident. It requires serious attention to developing a communication channel.

Assess Your Line Manager

  • Understand what they want for themselves and their values.
  • Analyse their style: Do they prefer written detailed reports, verbal briefings, or formal meetings?
  • Consider the pressures on them and their own dealings with senior managers.
  • What are their rewards for succeeding?

Understand Their Strengths & Weaknesses

  • Do they need lengthy explanations?
  • Are they good at one-to-one communication?
  • Are they able to handle conflict, or do they avoid it?

Action: Complement their weaknesses. Modify your behavior to ensure the relationship is legitimate and productive.

What a Manager Provides vs. What You Must Provide

Manager provides: Alternative views, overall picture information, advice on difficult issues, policy guidance, support, and protection.

You must provide: Clear documentation, defined issues, identified courses of action, reasoned arguments, recommendations, predictions of outcomes, and team progress.

7. Decision Making & Time Management

Key Skills for Management

Self-management, communication, time management, resource management, and decision making.

Qualities of a Good Manager (Self-Management)

  • Honesty and integrity.
  • Orderly and neat appearance.
  • Loyalty and conscientiousness.
  • Able to lead and set an example.
  • Willingness and cooperativeness.
  • Enthusiastic and punctual.
  • Courteous and caring.

Decision-Making Styles

  • Autocratic: Manager solves the problem based on their info.
  • Information finding: Manager asks others for info, then decides.
  • Consultation: Group generates solutions; leader decides.
  • Negotiation: Group negotiates an acceptable solution.
  • Delegation: Responsibility rests with a group/individual.

Steps to Effective Decision Making

  1. Classify the problem: Is it generic (use policy) or extraordinary (handle individually)?
  2. Define the problem: Check against all observable facts.
  3. Specify conditions: Clarify exactly what the decision must accomplish (boundary conditions).
  4. Decide on the right action: Do what is right, not just what is acceptable.
  5. Compromise: Make the best decision possible within circumstances.
  6. Implement: Assign responsibility and inform everyone.
  7. Review: Monitor and receive feedback.

Time Management

  • Plan each day, decide on priorities.
  • Identify immediate, short-term, and long-term goals.
  • Avoid distractions, prevent interruptions.
  • Delegate, make lists, and delete things as they are done.
  • Do important jobs when you are at your best.
  • Do not put off unpleasant tasks.
  • Learn to say no, and think before acting.

8. Managing People & Self-Assessment

Teams in Organisations

  • Formal teams: Departments/sections created to pursue specified goals.
  • Informal teams: Deal with particular situations, fewer fixed relationships, disbanded after function.

A good, developed team can analyse problems effectively, communicate, and show good leadership resulting in skilled operations.

Assessing Yourself as a Manager

To effectively manage people and resources, it is first essential to manage oneself. Analyse yourself truthfully, assess weaknesses, and take positive action.

Category / Type of Person Traits Management Skills That May Need Improving
